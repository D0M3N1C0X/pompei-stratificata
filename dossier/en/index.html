After AD 79
Pompeii and Herculaneum

Sixteen centuries between the eruption and the first stroke of the pick. They were not centuries of silence: they were centuries of digging without archaeology. And fifteen kilometres away, under twenty metres of hardened currents, the same night produced a history of excavation that cannot be reconciled with this one.

00 — Premise

The question has to be corrected before it can be answered

There was digging, plenty of it. People dug from the days immediately after the eruption and kept digging for centuries. What did not exist was not digging: it was digging in order to know.

Second correction: Domenico Fontana's works (1594–1600) were not an excavation. They were a hydraulic project that cut through the buried city without recognising it. The first intentional excavation at Pompeii dates to 23 March 1748 — and even that was not archaeology: it was a hunt for museum pieces for the royal palace at Portici.

Assessment — mine, open to discussion

The question that holds up in front of an examining committee is not “why did nobody dig”. It is: why, for sixteen centuries, did nobody have a reason to want to know what lay down there? It is a question of cultural history, not of archaeology — and it is the reason the project can interest a historian as much as a Latinist.

Five metres, and not everything

The consequence

Pompeii was never invisible: it became illegible. With five metres, a ground floor four metres high disappears and the upper floor does not. That is the physical reason the city remained within reach — of salvagers, of looters and, four centuries later, of those who came back to live there.

⚠️ Caveat

The thickness varies from one part of the city to another. The figure holds for the published sector, not for all of Pompeii. The maximum thickness of the fall deposit alone is 2.8–2.9 m.

02 — The ancient sources

What Suetonius and Cassius Dio actually say

This is where the classics professor will be waiting for you, because the two passages are constantly quoted to say more than they say.

Curatores restituendae Campaniae e consularium numero sorte duxit; bona oppressorum in Vesuvio, quorum heredes non exstabant, restitutioni afflictarum civitatum attribuit.

He chose by lot, from among the ex-consuls, commissioners for the restoration of Campania; the property of those who had perished on Vesuvius and left no heirs he assigned to the rebuilding of the stricken cities. Suet., Tit. 8.4

Cassius Dio confirms and adds: Titus sends “two ex-consuls to the Campanians to oversee the restoration of the region”, distributes money and assigns to the survivors the property of those who had died without heirs. Dio 66.24

The point that holds up in an exam

Dio names Pompeii and Herculaneum, but in the account of the destruction: “it buried two entire cities, Herculaneum and Pompeii” (66.23.3). In the account of the relief (66.24) and in Suetonius (8.4) the object of imperial intervention is Campania as a region and the afflictae civitates in general. No ancient source records an attempt to rebuild Pompeii. The help goes to the living, not to the ruins.

The rest of the literary tradition is almost silent

  • Pliny the Younger, Ep. 6.16 and 6.20: the eruption and his uncle's death. Not the aftermath.
  • Tacitus, Hist. 1.2: a mention; the full treatment is lost.
  • Josephus, Ant. Iud. 20.144: promises an account that has not reached us.
  • Martial 4.44 and Statius, Silv. 3.5.72–75; 4.4.78–85; 4.8.3–5; 5.3.205–208: elegiac register, not documentary. They are laments for a lost landscape.
Verified — the editions to check against

Do not quote Martial and Statius from online collections: the numbering drifts (I found pages giving Martial 4.44 as 4.4). These are the reference editions, and I verified their bibliographic details on the publishers' pages:

How to read it

The gap between the fifth and fifteenth centuries is not evidence of abandonment. It is the combined effect of two things: excavations that removed the post-79 phases to reach the level of 79, and the fact that nobody ever looked for those phases. The fifteenth-century glazed basin found in 2026 closes the gap at one end. The other end is still open — and that is research space, not a hole in the argument.

04 — Ancient digging

They dug at once: first to recover, then to loot

  • Recovery by the owners. Digging tools beside the bodies; tunnels dug from one room to the next; breaches in the walls of rooms that had been emptied.
  • Systematic stripping of materials. Marble, stone blocks and lead removed from the baths, the Forum, the theatres, the Amphitheatre. Parco Archeologico di Pompei, Pompei dopo l'eruzione
  • Ancient tomb robbers, documented by excavation. In the Casa con Giardino (Regio V) at least six individuals were found “turned over, twisted and dragged” by people looking for objects; rings that the looters had missed were torn from the bodies. PAP, press release of 24 October 2018
Why it matters

It is not colour. It is proof that someone knew where the city was, knew there was something in it and knew how to get there. Sixteen centuries of “oblivion” do not stand up against an emptied room.

The domus pertusa case — and how to cite it

At the entrance of the House of N. Popidius Priscus (VII.2.20), on the south side of the Vicolo del Panettiere, Fiorelli recorded a graffito scratched with a nail and interpreted it as the sign left by those who had already emptied the building, placing the operation “probably in the age of the Antonines”.

⚠️ Correction from the previous version

The graffito was in Greek. Domus pertusa is not the text of the inscription: it is the Latin equivalent Fiorelli gave for it. The sources I could reach do not reproduce the Greek text. If you write “the domus pertusa graffito” to an epigraphist, this is the first thing you will be told.

I found no trace of a CIL IV number. The Ancient Graffiti Project records no graffiti for this property, but its silence proves nothing: for Pompeii the database covers only the Lupanar, the palaestra campus, Regio I insula 8, Regio V insula 1 and all of Regio VIII — Regio VII is not covered. The place to look is the printed CIL IV and its De Gruyter supplements edited by Solin, Varone and Kruschwitz; fascicle 4.2 (2021) covers the commentary on CIL IV 1205–7108, 4.3 appeared in 2023 and 4.4 is not yet out.

05 — The revision under way

Pompeii was reinhabited, and archaeology erased the evidence

This is the most recent part and the most interesting to present, because it is not a curiosity: it is a methodological revision published by the Park itself. Stratigraphic excavation in the Insula meridionalis documents two post-eruption phases.

  • Late first – early third century — African Red Slip ware in Hayes forms 5–23, reused marble, a coin of the age of Marcus Aurelius (AD 161).
  • Fourth – fifth century — stable settlement: bread ovens built into cisterns, hearths, hand mills, lamps with the Constantinian monogram, folles of 326, burials of infants and of equids.

After 79, Pompeii re-emerges less as a city than as a precarious, grey agglomeration, a kind of camp, a favela among ruins that were still recognisable.

Gabriel Zuchtriegel, director of the Parco Archeologico di Pompei (my translation from the Italian)

The methodological thesis — the “archaeological unconscious”

The authors argue that the post-79 traces were literally removed by earlier excavations, because every campaign aimed to reach the level of AD 79. The documentation that would answer your question was destroyed by the discipline that should have collected it. For a presentation, this is a stronger theme than the eruption itself.

⚠️ Conflict narrowed, not closed

On the 2026 article the name Giuseppe Barbella is not a byline in the text: it appears in the meta-author field of the web page, that is, in the metadata of the Park's CMS. The same holds for “Antonio Benforte” on the 2025 article, whose PDF instead carries the five authors I cite. For 2026 the specialist press (Archaeoreporter, 21 August 2026) attributes the work to Borsa, Motisi, Ortino, Salvatori, Zuchtriegel; Naòs and Artribune give no list of authors. The PDF is over 30 MB and my tools do not open it.

What to do: open the PDF, look at the header of the first page, cite that. It is the only step that settles the question, and it takes a minute.

07 — The causes

Why nobody dug in order to know

I

What could be recovered had already been recovered

Between survivors, imperial authorities and looters, whatever could be sold or reused left the site within a few centuries. Those who came later had no economic incentive to dig.

II

The place was not forgotten: the name was lost

The hill went on being called Civita; the Tabula Peutingeriana shows Pompeis between Oplontis and Nuceria/Stabiae; and now fifteenth-century pottery proves the site was frequented. What went missing was not the place, but the link between that place and that name.

III

The practice of excavating for knowledge did not exist

Until modern antiquarianism, ruins were a quarry for materials or a store of curiosities, not a source of knowledge. Britannica itself dates “the beginning of modern archaeological science” to the mid-eighteenth-century work on these cities: before then the gesture was not available.

IV

When they did dig, they dug for the king

The stated aim of the Bourbon works: to locate and extract works of art and artefacts to decorate the royal palace at Portici. Alcubierre at first believed he was at Stabiae. Parslow 1995

⚠️ A cause I had to withdraw

The previous version had a fifth cause: “the burial was reinforced by later eruptions” — Pollena 472 and Vesuvius 1631. I checked it and it does not hold.

De Simone, Perrotta & Scarpati 2011 study only two sites, both on the northern slope (Pollena Trocchia and Somma Vesuviana): Pompeii is not among them. The Osservatorio Vesuviano of INGV places the dispersal of 472 in the north-western and south-eastern sectors, and that of 1631 on the southern and western flanks, with pyroclastic flows reaching the sea at Torre del Greco and Torre Annunziata; Rosi, Principe & Vecci 1993 confirm Torre Annunziata (7.5 km from the crater) and Torre del Greco (8.5 km). None of these sources identifies Pompeii as an area of deposition.

What remains true of 1631 is something else, and it should be put this way: after that eruption the canal works and interest in the area were abandoned. It is a fact about the history of attention, not about stratigraphy.

08 — The research gap

Nobody has published what lies above AD 79

In trying to verify the withdrawn cause, something more interesting than the cause itself came up. The question “what lies above the AD 79 level inside the walls of Pompeii — tephra from later eruptions? colluvium? agricultural soil? and how thick?” has no published answer that I have been able to find.

The only peer-reviewed statement that comes close is in a seismic microzonation study of the Park: for the unexcavated areas the authors write “we assume that there are ca. 5 m of 79 AD pyroclastic deposits and more recent soils”. It is a declared assumption, not a stratigraphic datum: the “more recent soils” are neither dated nor attributed.

Amato, Covolan, Dessales, Santoriello 2022, Geosciences 12(7), 275 · DOI 10.3390/geosciences12070275

How to use it in a research project

A precisely named gap is worth more than ten pages of literature review. Phrase it like this: there is no geoarchaeological study of the stratigraphic column above the AD 79 level within the urban perimeter, and without one it is impossible to say whether the city was progressively covered, eroded or stable for sixteen centuries. It connects directly to the “archaeological unconscious” of Zuchtriegel and colleagues: that column is exactly what the excavations removed.

Before declaring it in a proposal, though, do a serious bibliographic check — in databases, not search engines. An exhaustive web search is not one.

09 — Herculaneum

The same eruption, two histories of excavation

Herculaneum is not Pompeii's control case: it is the case that makes visible what, at Pompeii, depends on the deposit and not on people. Same night, same volcano, fifteen kilometres apart — and two incompatible archaeologies.

Why the burial is different

At Pompeii the Plinian fallout comes first: hours of pumice falling from the sky, which give people time to leave their houses. The pyroclastic density currents come later. At Herculaneum that phase is missing: the dispersal axis of the column points south-east, towards Pompeii, and the town on the sea receives pyroclastic density currents from the start.

The result is a deposit of a different nature and an incomparable thickness. The Parco Archeologico di Ercolano writes, verbatim, that the Bourbon excavators had to get through “approximately 20 m of volcanic subsoil to reach the floor of the ancient city”.

Pensa, Capra, Giordano, Corrado 2023, Scientific Reports 13, 5622 · DOI 10.1038/s41598-023-32623-3 · Parco Archeologico di Ercolano, La scoperta e gli scavi

⚠️ The sources disagree

There is no single figure for the thickness. Maiuri (1960) writes “16 and more metres above the level of the ancient city” (my translation); the Park says “approximately 20 m”; D'Andrea and others (2019), working on the GIS of the Herculaneum Conservation Project, write “often up to 30 metre below the modern town”. The 3D model draws 20 m, the modal value, and the conflict is declared there as it is here. I do not resolve it: the three figures measure slightly different things (deposit above the ancient level, total deposit, depth below the modern surface) and no source makes this explicit.

Twenty metres bury worse and preserve better

It is the paradox worth saying out loud, because it overturns intuition. The pyroclastic currents at Herculaneum carbonise instead of burning: they deprive wood of oxygen and turn it into charcoal without consuming it. From there come beams, partitions, doors, furniture, and the Casa a Graticcio — a building in opus craticium, a timber frame filled with reeds and mortar, cheap Roman construction, which at Pompeii simply would not survive.

The same chemistry gives us the carbonised papyrus rolls of the Villa of the Papyri: the only library of classical antiquity to have reached us as a physical body, and not through medieval manuscript copies.

Assessment — mine, open to discussion

This is why the comparison is not decorative. If Pompeii is the city where you see the plan and Herculaneum the one where you see the material, then the dossier's question — why nobody dug in order to know — receives a different and sharper answer at Herculaneum: there, nobody dug in order to know because, literally, open excavation was impossible. They dug by tunnel, as in a mine, and a tunnel gives back objects, not contexts.

The beach and the vaulted chambers

Beneath the terraces of the Sacred Area and of Marcus Nonius Balbus, at the level of the ancient shoreline, open twelve vaulted rooms — six west and six east of the stairway — interpreted as stores for fishing gear and shelters for small boats.

Since the 1980s human remains have been found there, in nine of the twelve rooms: people who did not manage to get away by sea. With them, jewellery, baskets, purses with coins, house keys, amulets, work tools, a surgical kit. On the beach, a wooden boat and the collapsed pronaos of the temple of Venus.

Parco Archeologico di Ercolano, I fuggiaschi · Pappalardo 1994, Enciclopedia dell'Arte Antica · Guidobaldi & Esposito 2013, pp. 21–26 · Martyn, Craig, Ellingham, Islam, Fattore, Sperduti, Bondioli, Thompson 2020, Antiquity 94(373), 76–91 · DOI 10.15184/aqy.2019.215

⚠️ The number of dead is not a stable figure

The Archaeological Park gives about 300 victims in the vaulted chambers. Pappalardo, in 1994, wrote 230 in all, some sixty of them on the beach itself. The English Wikipedia entry gives a progression: 55 in the first six rooms (1980–82), then 296, then “eventually 340 bodies”. Part of the difference is almost certainly the year of publication and not a disagreement of substance — but no source says so, so I do not present it as resolved. If you cite a figure, cite the date of the source too.

Who dug, and how

c. 1709

The digging of a well strikes ancient structures: the accidental discovery associated with the name of the Prince d'Elbeuf, who from there began collecting marbles. To be verified: the Park's official page that I read does not report this episode or its date, and I have not checked a primary source.

1738

“In 1738, at the behest of the King of Naples, Charles of Bourbon, the first systematic explorations of Herculaneum's subsoil began through a network of shafts and tunnels which made it possible to overcome the approximately 20 m of volcanic subsoil to reach the floor of the ancient city.” The first monument reached is the theatre. Excavation at Herculaneum begins ten years before Pompeii — and it begins underground.

1754

Karl Weber draws the plan of the Villa of the Papyri, surveyed through tunnels. It is the document that still guides investigation in that sector today, because the villa remains largely buried.

1927

“it was only in 1927 that the new excavations of Herculaneum began, under the direction of Amedeo Maiuri”: the start of open-air excavation, which produced the area that can be walked today. To be verified: the year the campaign ended. The sources I read give different figures and I have not found which is correct.

1980s

“In the 1980s, there was a new impetus for excavations at Herculaneum that unearthed the ancient shoreline, leading to the discovery of the skeletons of the escapees and a boat.” Until then it was believed that almost all the inhabitants had managed to flee: it is one of the sharpest corrections produced by any excavation in the twentieth century.

All quotations in quotation marks: Parco Archeologico di Ercolano, La scoperta e gli scavi (English version, read on 30 August 2026) · Weber 1754: Ministero della cultura, Villa dei Papiri entry

How much is excavated, and why the rest is not

The Decumanus Maximus is the boundary: it “serves today as the line between the excavated area and the area still buried beneath corso Resina” (my translation). Corso Resina is the old Strada Regia delle Calabrie, the road to Naples. Beneath it lie the rest of the town, the theatre, the Villa of the Papyri.

According to the Park, “more than four” hectares of the town are visible: four whole insulae plus Insulae Orientalis I and II, and parts of II and VII. Of the five cardines three can be seen — the third, fourth and fifth; of the three decumani, two.

⚠️ Two conflicts over size

Extent of the town. The Park calculates it “hypothetically at about twenty hectares”. Maiuri in 1960 gave a rectangle of 370 × 320 m, “an area, including the extramural buildings, of no more than 11–12 hectares”. The two figures cannot be reconciled; Maiuri's is internally consistent (370 × 320 = ~11.8 ha) and is the only metric measurement I have found published.

Excavated area. “More than four hectares” for the Park; “areas totalling some 60.000 square metres” — six hectares — in the GIS work of the Herculaneum Conservation Project. Neither source explains the difference. It is plausible that the second includes sectors not open to the public, but that is my conjecture, not a datum.

The reason the rest is not excavated is not archaeological. UNESCO puts it in writing: the integrity of the site “would be improved by inclusion within the property… of the theatre and the largest part of the ancient town with its most significant public monuments still lying beneath the modern Ercolano”.

There is a living town on top. Excavating Herculaneum means expropriating and demolishing Ercolano — the municipality that, by Presidential Decree no. 40 of 12 February 1969, stopped being called Resina and took the name of the city beneath it.

The thesis, in one line

Pompeii became illegible. Herculaneum became unreachable. At Pompeii five metres of deposit leave the upper floors and roofs exposed: the city remains within reach of salvagers, looters and those who come back to live there, but stops being understandable as a city. At Herculaneum twenty metres of hardened currents close everything: nobody comes back to live above the ruins; a new town is built on top without knowing what lies beneath. Two different ways of disappearing, from the same night.

What I have not verified about Herculaneum

The same rule applies as in the rest of the dossier: what I have not checked is written down.

⚠️ Declared gaps

The orientation of the street grid. The Park and Maiuri give the decumani parallel to the coast, with the sea to the west and the axis NW–SE; a British university page gives the coastal road running NE–SW. I have not resolved it. The 3D model is therefore built in the site convention — Decumanus Maximus uphill, sea downhill — and not in true north. A georeferenced plan with a stated azimuth would close the gap.

The height of the excavation face as such, as distinct from the thickness of the deposit. I found no published figure. It would take a section from the Herculaneum Conservation Project or the geotechnical reports from the consolidation works on the slope.

The present distance of the site from the sea. No source, at any level.

The consistency of the deposit. “Hard tuff” is the phrase that circulates everywhere, but the closest peer-reviewed source I read (Camardo 2007) says compact, not rock. If it needs saying, say it this way: “described as compact, its hardness making excavation more laborious than at Pompeii”, and mark it as to be verified.

Basilica, Augusteum, Basilica Noniana. The Park's official map lists “Basilica” and “Augusteum” as two separate entries; other sources treat the two names as synonyms for the same building, and place on Cardo III a third building called the Basilica Noniana. Three labels, at least two buildings, no source that reconciles them. In the model I have included only the Basilica Noniana.

10 — From the canal to the name

The rediscovery, step by step

1592

Count Muzio Tuttavilla commissions Domenico Fontana to build the Canale del Conte, to bring water to the mills of Torre Annunziata. The route crosses the hill of Civita.

1594–1600

While the canal is being dug, coins and the remains of buildings come to light. Nobody realises this is the Roman city. Giunti 1983, pp. 126–127

1631

Eruption of Vesuvius. The works and interest in the area are abandoned.

1738

Excavation begins at Herculaneum under Rocque Joaquín de Alcubierre, at the wish of Charles of Bourbon: tunnels more than twenty metres deep in compacted volcanic material.

23 Mar 1748

First systematic works at Civita. Part of the Amphitheatre and the necropolis of the Herculaneum Gate are located. The works are then suspended for lack of valuable finds.

1750–1764

Karl Jakob Weber introduces plans and weekly inventories of the finds: the first documentation worthy of the name. Parslow 1995

1763

The discovery of the inscription rei publicae Pompeianorum finally identifies the site. 1684 years have passed since the eruption and 15 since the start of excavation.

1860

Giuseppe Fiorelli takes over the direction and introduces the stratigraphic method and the numbering by regiones and insulae.

2018

Regio V: the Casa con Giardino documents the ancient looters; the charcoal inscription reopens the debate on the date of the eruption.

2023 →

Consolidation works in the Insula Meridionalis. The evidence published in 2025 and 2026 comes from there.

11 — The trap

A widespread thesis that does not hold

An explanation circulates that in the sixteenth century nobody dug because it would have been “a crime against religion to unearth a city destroyed” by divine wrath. It is a line that works very well in a presentation.

⚠️ Do not use it

The phrase goes back to Domenico Murano, a nineteenth-century military hydraulic engineer, in a work on the Sarno Canal. It is not a sixteenth-century document: it is a nineteenth-century historiographical topos. If you bring it to a history professor as an explanation of sixteenth-century events, you will be asked for the primary source and you do not have one. At most you can use it as an object of study: as an example of how the nineteenth century told itself the story of the silence of earlier centuries.

The five checks, and how they went

Two corrections, one clarification, two questions that remain open with a note on where to close them.

CheckOutcomeWhat changes
CIL number of the domus pertusa graffito Correction The graffito was in Greek; domus pertusa is Fiorelli's Latin rendering. No CIL number found. To be searched in the printed CIL IV: the Ancient Graffiti Project does not cover Regio VII.
Deposits of 472 and 1631 on Pompeii Correction Cause withdrawn. No scientific source documents contributions from those eruptions on the urban site. In exchange, a gap in the literature emerged, now §08.
Critical editions of Martial and Statius Resolved Lindsay (OCT) and Shackleton Bailey (Loeb 94, 1993) for Martial; Courtney (OCT, 1990) for the Silvae. Details verified on the publishers' pages.
Authors of the E-Journal 2026.09 article Open “Giuseppe Barbella” is the CMS meta-author field, not a byline. Archaeoreporter gives five names; no other outlet confirms. Close it yourself by opening the PDF.

What I have not verified

An explicit list, because it is the part that protects you. Updated to v3.

  • The Greek text of the VII.2.20 graffito and its CIL IV number. To be looked for in the printed Corpus and the De Gruyter supplements.
  • The attribution of the 2026 article: the PDF exceeds 30 MB and my tools do not open it.
  • The route of the Canale del Conte under the city, and the version according to which Fontana struck frescoes and inscriptions. In the 3D model the route is schematic and declared as such. Rispoli & Paone 2011 is the first source to open.
  • Rolandi, Barrella & Borrelli 1993 on the 1631 eruption (JVGR 58, 183–201): I could not access it. It might mention Pompeii — I cannot rule that out.
  • The verbatim quotations from Sulpizio et al. 2007 and Rosi et al. 1993 come from automatic extraction of PDF copies, not from direct reading of the original.
  • The gap declared in §08 rests on a web search, not on a bibliographic database search. Before putting it in a proposal, check it again on Scopus, Web of Science or L'Année philologique.
  • I have not directly consulted Parslow 1995, Cooley & Cooley 2014, or Tuck 2020/2025: I verified their existence and bibliographic details, not their content page by page.
